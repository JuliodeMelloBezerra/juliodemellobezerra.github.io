CurveSmash · BLS12-381 · Special Tower Number Field Sieve (Part 1)

Smashing the Tower Number Field Sieve

How the Tower Number Field Sieve (TNFS) dominated progress across three cryptanalysis tracks on BLS12-381; and how the CurveSmash benchmark established a new gold standard for rigor and cost measurement while driving substantial algorithmic improvements to the attack itself—fixing a broken polynomial and pushing the verified cost down to 2115.70.

By Julio de Mello Bezerra and Youssef El Housni

Remark · Post-06/11 AdvancementsThis article was originally written based on CurveSmash submissions up to 06/11 (which reached 2119.48). Major advancements occurred in the subsequent days (06/11–08/11), driving the verified cost down to 2115.70 (2115.6983). These post-06/11 breakthroughs are briefly summarized in the timeline ladder and math deep-dive in Section 04.

01 · One Sieve to Rule Three TracksWhy a finite-field DLP oracle breaks both curves and pairings

The CurveSmash challenge on Yukon replaces asymptotic hand-waving with containerized, instruction-metered cryptanalysis. Every submission must supply a complete mathematical proof alongside executable C, AArch64 assembly, and Python verification scripts audited via Callgrind.

Of CurveSmash’s six tracks, three are governed by elliptic-curve group attacks (Pollard’s \(\rho\) and Cheon’s algorithm). The best submissions on the other three tracks are all powered by a single finite-field engine: the Special Tower Number Field Sieve (STNFS) on BLS12-381:

  • Plain \(G_1\) DLP (bls12-381-g1-ecdlp-plain): Given \(P_0 \in G_1\) and \(T = [x]P_0\), recover the \(255\)-bit scalar \(x \in \mathbb{F}_r\).
  • Fixed-Argument Pairing Inversion 1 — FAPI-1 (bls12-381-fixed-g1-invert-g2): Given \(P \in G_1 \setminus \{\mathcal{O}\}\) and \(z \in G_T\), find \(Q \in G_2\) such that \(e(P, Q) = z\).
  • Fixed-Argument Pairing Inversion 2 — FAPI-2 (bls12-381-fixed-g2-invert-g1): Given \(Q \in G_2 \setminus \{\mathcal{O}\}\) and \(z \in G_T\), find \(P \in G_1\) such that \(e(P, Q) = z\).

Attacking \(G_1\) or \(G_2\) directly on the curve requires \(\approx \sqrt{\pi r / 4} \approx 2^{127.2}\) group operations via Pollard’s \(\rho\). However, the optimal ate pairing \(e : G_1 \times G_2 \to G_T \subset \mathbb{F}_{p^{12}}^*\) is bilinear: \(e([a]P, [b]Q) = e(P, Q)^{ab}\). It acts as a bridge that transports problems from the elliptic curve into the \(4569\)-bit extension field \(\mathbb{F}_{p^{12}}^*\)—where index calculus runs orders of magnitude faster.

Plain DLP on \(G_1\) (MOV Reduction): Pick any fixed generator \(Q_0 \in G_2 \setminus \{\mathcal{O}\}\) and compute \(z_0 = e(P_0, Q_0)\) and \(z_T = e(T, Q_0) = e([x]P_0, Q_0) = z_0^x\) in \(G_T\). Solving the discrete log \(z_T = z_0^x\) in \(\mathbb{F}_{p^{12}}^*\) directly yields \(x \pmod r\).

2. FAPI-1 (Invert \(G_2\) for fixed \(P \in G_1\)): Pick any generator \(Q_0 \in G_2\), compute \(z_0 = e(P, Q_0) \in G_T\), and use the STNFS oracle to find \(y \in \mathbb{F}_r\) such that \(z = z_0^y\). Return \(Q = [y]Q_0 \in G_2\), which satisfies \(e(P, Q) = e(P, Q_0)^y = z_0^y = z\).

3. FAPI-2 (Invert \(G_1\) for fixed \(Q \in G_2\)): Symmetrically pick \(P_0 \in G_1 \setminus \{\mathcal{O}\}\), set \(z_0 = e(P_0, Q)\), solve \(z = z_0^x\) in \(G_T\), and return \(P = [x]P_0 \in G_1\).

02 · From NFS to TNFS to STNFSLifting to a 12-dimensional number field tower

In a finite field \(\mathbb{F}_{p^{12}}^*\), arithmetic wraps around modulo \(p\), destroying any notion of “size” or unique factorization into small primes. Index calculus restores factorization by lifting the problem to characteristic zero: we construct two algebraic number rings \(\mathcal{O}_{K_f}\) and \(\mathcal{O}_{K_g}\) that share a common homomorphism down to \(\mathbb{F}_{p^{12}}\). Up in these rings, elements factor uniquely into prime ideals, whose size is measured by their integer norm \(N(\cdot) \in \mathbb{Z}\). Fixing a smoothness bound \(B\), we collect all prime ideals of norm \(< B\) on both sides into a factor base of size \(U \approx \text{Li}(B)\).

Whenever a sieved candidate pair \((a, b)\), \(a - bX\), generates principal ideals whose integer norms \(N_f, N_g \in \mathbb{Z}\) factor completely into \(B\)-smooth primes (primes \(q < B\)), pushing both factorizations down to \(\mathbb{F}_{p^{12}}^*\) equates the two sides. Because prime ideals upstairs are not elements of \(\mathbb{F}_{p^{12}}^*\) directly, we assign each factor-base ideal \(\mathfrak{p}\) an unknown weight modulo \(r\) called its virtual logarithm \(\log \mathfrak{p}\). Each doubly \(B\)-smooth pair then yields one linear equation modulo \(r\) connecting those \(U\) unknowns. To obtain a full-rank system that can be solved directly via sparse linear algebra (Block Wiedemann), we must collect more distinct smooth relations \(R_{\text{found}}\) than factor-base unknowns \(U\):

$$R_{\text{found}} \;=\; V \cdot E \;>\; U \;\approx\; \text{Li}(B),$$

where \(V\) is the usable sieving volume and \(E\) is Murphy’s \(E\)—the average probability over the sieve region that a candidate is simultaneously \(B\)-smooth on both sides. Accurately estimating \(V \cdot E\) versus \(U\)—and above all the joint smoothness probability \(E\)—is by far the hardest part of analyzing the sieve.

Why build a tower? Classical NFS lifts \(\mathbb{F}_{p^{12}}\) in a single degree-\(12\) jump over \(\mathbb{Q}\) and sieves integer pairs \((a, b) \in \mathbb{Z}^2\), forcing \(|a|, |b| \approx 2^{60}\) and causing the norms to explode. The Tower Number Field Sieve (TNFS)[1] mirrors the composite extension \(12 = \eta \cdot \kappa = 6 \cdot 2\)—it first builds a degree-\(6\) base field \(K_h = \mathbb{Q}(y)\), and then sieves degree-\(12\) polynomials \(a - bX\) whose coefficients \(a, b\) are polynomials in \(y\) inside \(\mathcal{O}_{K_h} \cong \mathbb{Z}^6\). Spreading the search volume \(V\) across \(12\) dimensions shrinks each integer coordinate to \(A \approx 600\) (\(< 10\) bits).

How the curve seed u powers the Special TNFS (STNFS)

Even with a tower (\(\eta = 6, \kappa = 2\)), a generic prime \(p\) forces the top polynomials \(f_y, g_y\) to have huge coefficients (on the order of \(p^{1/3}\)). The Special TNFS (STNFS) exploits the fact that BLS12-381’s \(381\)-bit prime \(p\) is parameterized by a \(64\)-bit integer seed \(u\) via \(3p = \Gamma(u) = u^6 - 2u^5 + 2u^3 + u + 1\). Setting \(g_y(X) = X^2 - uy\) (degree \(2\)) and substituting \(u = X^2/y\) into \(\Gamma(u)\) gives a degree-\(12\) polynomial \(f_y(X) = y^6\Gamma(X^2/y) \bmod h(y)\) whose coefficients drop from \(\sim 2^{127}\) down to \(1\) bit (\(\|f_y\|_\infty = 2\)).

BLS12-381 Polynomials, Infinite Units & The Schirokauer Mapclick to expand

Curve Seed & Polynomials: BLS12-381 is parameterized by a \(64\)-bit seed \(u\), with \(381\)-bit base prime \(3p = \Gamma(u) := u^6 - 2u^5 + 2u^3 + u + 1\) and \(255\)-bit subgroup prime \(r = u^4 - u^2 + 1\). With @gbotrel’s base polynomial \(h(Y) = Y^6 + Y^3 + Y^2 - Y - 1\), the top polynomials are:

$$g_y(X) = X^2 - uy, \qquad f_y(X) = y^6 \Gamma(X^2/y) \bmod h(y) = X^{12} - 2yX^{10} + 2y^3X^6 + y^5X^2 + y^6 + y^5 + 1.$$

Why Infinite Units Create Redundant Ideals: In \(\mathbb{Z}\), the only invertible elements (units) are \(\{\pm 1\}\). In a number ring \(\mathcal{O}_K\), however, there may be infinitely many units \(\varepsilon \in \mathcal{O}_K^\times\) (as is the case in our rings \(\mathcal{O}_{K_h}, \mathcal{O}_{K_f}, \mathcal{O}_{K_g}\)). Multiplying any candidate pair \((a, b)\) by a unit \(\varepsilon \in \mathcal{O}_{K_h}^\times\) produces a different integer tuple inside the sieve region that generates the exact same principal ideal—yielding a redundant duplicate relation that adds zero rank to the linear system.

How the Schirokauer Map Resolves Unit Ambiguity: Even when a principal ideal factors uniquely into prime ideals \(\prod \mathfrak{p}_i^{e_i}\), ideal equality only determines the element up to an unknown unit factor \(u \in \mathcal{O}_K^\times\). Computing fundamental units explicitly in degree-72 fields is infeasible; instead, STNFS uses Schirokauer maps—efficiently computable linear maps modulo \(r\) evaluated directly on the polynomial coefficients—to append a few dozen extra columns to the matrix that automatically absorb all unit contributions modulo \(r\).

03 · Pre-CurveSmash Estimates & The Accounting RulerWhy 117–120 bits (B-D), 126.0 bits (G-S), and 115.70 bits (CurveSmash) use different rulers

When Kim and Barbulescu introduced the Extended TNFS (ExTNFS)[1] for composite extension degrees—and combined a degree-\(6\) (sextic) base field with the special polynomial construction for \(p = \Gamma(u)\) to form SexTNFS (or the less sexy STNFS)—the security of BN-254 dropped below \(110\) bits. Drawing on the BLS family of pairing-friendly curves introduced by Barreto, Lynn, and Scott[2], Sean Bowe instantiated BLS12-381 in 2017 for Zcash’s Sapling upgrade. Two landmark cryptanalytic papers then defined its pre-CurveSmash security estimates: Barbulescu–Duquesne (B-D, 2019)[3], which estimated BLS12-381 at \(117\text{–}120\) bits (as cited in [4][5]), and Guillevic–Singh (G-S, 2021)[6], which refined the number-theoretic model and raised the estimate to \(126.0\) bits.

Comparing those figures to CurveSmash’s \(123.61 \to 119.48 \to 115.70\) bits requires untangling two large effects (~7–8 bits each) that pull in opposite directions:

  • 1. The Unit-Conversion Discount (\(\approx -7.1\) bits numerically): B-D and G-S count abstract elementary operations (\(\approx 1\) cycle or \(\ln\ln B \approx 4.19\) ops per sieved cell). CurveSmash measures compiled AArch64 instructions and divides by \(C = 20{,}000 \approx 2^{14.29}\) instructions (one \(\mathbb{F}_{p^{12}}\) multiplication on the FAPI tracks, or \(C = 2{,}000\) on the plain \(G_1\) DLP track). Merely re-denominating G-S’s \(126.0\) abstract bits into CurveSmash’s ruler lowers the numerical score by \(\sim 7.1\) bits to \(\approx 118.9\) CS bits (and B-D to \(\approx 108\text{–}112\) CS bits).
  • 2. The Missing-Mathematics Penalty (\(\approx +7\text{ to }+8\) bits of real work): B-D and G-S sieved a 12D hypercube \([-A, A]^{12}\) without noticing that \(88\%\) of smooth pairs in the box are free-unit duplicates \((\varepsilon a, \varepsilon b)\) under \(\mathcal{O}_{K_h}^\times\) (\(+3.05\) bits). They also assumed \(100\%\) zero-loss threshold/ECM recovery (\(+0.3\) bits), hardcoded optimistic filtering factors at \(1.0\times\) excess (\(+1.85\) bits), and omitted the \(2\)-target individual logarithm \(\mathfrak{q}\)-descent, which forces the smoothness bound up from \(B = 2^{65.3}\) to \(B = 2^{69}\) (\(+2.3\) bits).

When you apply both adjustments to G-S’s box-sieve model, the \(-7.1\)-bit ruler discount and the \(+7.5\)-bit missing-math penalty cancel out—putting a valid, corrected G-S box-sieve attack at \(\sim 126.4\) CurveSmash bits (\(\sim 133.5\) abstract G-S bits). CurveSmash contributors then invented real algorithmic breakthroughs that drove that true cost down to \(119.48\) CS bits by Nov 6 and \(115.70\) CS bits by Nov 8.

Guillevic–Singh’s Refinements Over Barbulescu–Duquesne · Coprimality ζ(2) & Murphy’s αclick to expand

The following two number-theoretic refinements were introduced by Guillevic and Singh (G-S, 2021)[6] and were completely absent from Barbulescu and Duquesne (B-D, 2019)[3]:

1. Coprimality & the \(1/\zeta_{K_h}(2)\) Factor Shrinking \(V\): In classical NFS over \(\mathbb{Z}\), if a pair of integers \((a,b)\) shares a prime factor \(\ell\), then \((a/\ell, b/\ell)\) generates the exact same relation, so we must restrict the sieve volume to coprime pairs \(\gcd(a,b) = 1\). Because a prime \(\ell\) divides \(a\) with probability \(1/\ell\) and divides both \(a\) and \(b\) independently with probability \(1/\ell^2\), the probability that no prime divides both is given by the Euler product over all primes:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(\gcd(a,b) = 1) &= \prod_{\ell\text{ prime}} \left(1 - \frac{1}{\ell^2}\right) = \left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^2}\right)^{-1} \\ &= \frac{1}{\zeta(2)} = \frac{6}{\pi^2} \approx 60.8\%, \end{aligned}$$

where \(\zeta(s)\) is the Riemann zeta function. Every number field \(K_h\) has an analogous Dedekind zeta function \(\zeta_{K_h}(s)\), and the probability that \((a,b)\) generate coprime ideals works analogously as \(1/\zeta_{K_h}(2)\). This factor directly multiplies (shrinks) the usable sieve volume \(V\) in \(R_{\text{found}} = V \cdot E\), making the attack less effective (whereas B-D ignored this factor).

2. Murphy’s \(\alpha\) (Small-Prime Bias vs. Uniform Random Integers): Values produced by a polynomial resultant \(N_f(a,b)\) do not behave like uniform random integers of the same size when it comes to small prime factors. Murphy’s \(\alpha\) measures this departure from uniform random divisibility across small primes. B-D assumed \(\alpha = 0\) (treating norms as independent uniform random integers), whereas G-S adapted Murphy’s \(\alpha\) to TNFS and showed that on BLS12-381, the rigid seed-derived polynomials have \(\alpha_f + \alpha_g \approx +4.53\) bits—meaning their norms are depleted of small prime factors, lowering \(E\) and adding bits to the attack cost.

04 · Fixing the Tower & Breaking 2116From G-S’s split quadratic to 119.48 → 115.70 CurveSmash bits

When CurveSmash contributors set out to implement Guillevic and Singh’s \(2^{126}\) attack in executable code, they immediately hit a wall: the base polynomial \(h_{\text{GS}}(Y) = Y^6 - Y^2 + 1\) selected by G-S (Table 11) for BLS12-381 is irreducible both over \(\mathbb{Z}\) and modulo \(p\) — but the tower it builds still collapses. The defect sits one level up, in \(g_y\). In the sextic field \(\mathbb{F}_p[Y]/(h_{\text{GS}}(Y)) = \mathbb{F}_{p^6}\), the element \(u\,y\) is a quadratic residue, so \(g_y = X^2 - u\,y\) factors over \(\mathbb{F}_{p^6}\): the degree-\(12\) extension never forms and the tower drops to degree \(6\). The failure survives the obvious check on \(h\) and only shows up as a Legendre symbol computed inside the sextic extension — Euler’s criterion gives \((u\,y)^{(p^6-1)/2} = +1\). Contributor @gbotrel fixed the foundation by finding \(h(Y) = Y^6 + Y^3 + Y^2 - Y - 1\), for which \(u\,y\) is a non-residue (\((u\,y)^{(p^6-1)/2} = -1\)), so \(g_y\) stays irreducible and \(\mathbb{F}_{p^{12}}\) is built correctly. Changing \(h\) repairs a defect that lives in \(g\) precisely because it changes which field \(\mathbb{F}_{p^6}\) is, and hence whether \(u\,y\) is square in it.

Second, instead of selecting candidate pairs \((a, b)\) inside a 12-dimensional hypercube \([-A, A]^{12}\) as in B-D and G-S, @gbotrel reshaped the sieving region into a 12D fibred ellipsoid \(\{Q_a(a) + Q_b(b) \le A^2\}\) (where \(Q_a, Q_b\) are positive-definite quadratic forms derived from the complex embeddings of \(K_h\)). Because the basis powers \(1, y, \dots, y^5\) have unequal sizes under the complex embeddings of \(K_h\), the sharp corners of a 12D hypercube extend far along directions where the algebraic norms \(N_f, N_g\) grow needlessly large; rounding the region into an embedding-matched ellipsoid reduces the average norm product by \(\sim 65\) bits at the exact same volume \(V\).

Third, rather than sieving every prime all the way up to the smoothness bound \(B = 2^{69}\)—which would require tracking an impossibly large factor base in memory—the sieve only processes primes up to a smaller bound \(X_2 = 2^{63}\). Whenever a candidate’s remaining cofactor after sieving is small enough to potentially split into primes in \([2^{63}, 2^{69}]\), it is factored on the fly using fast Suyama Montgomery Elliptic Curve Method (ECM) cofactorization.

Fourth, because @gbotrel’s ring \(\mathbb{Z}[y]\) has small index in \(\mathcal{O}_{K_h}\) — so Kummer–Dedekind applies at every odd prime, and a fixed integral basis suffices (full monogenicity is convenient but not required; the best polynomials found later are not monogenic either) — every prime ideal is a concrete \(6\)-dimensional integer lattice that can be written in \(6 \times 6\) Hermite Normal Form (HNF). Instead of relying on coarse asymptotic heuristics like \(1/\zeta_{K_h}(2)\), contributors used exact \(6 \times 6\) HNF lattices to enumerate ideals and obtain much more accurate, certified estimates for coprimality and Murphy’s \(\alpha\). From the initial valid baseline of \(123.61\) bits, open-source contributors shaved off \(4.13\) bits by Nov 6 to break \(2^{120}\) (\(119.48\) bits), and \(7.91\) bits (\(\approx 240\times\) less work) by Nov 8 to reach \(115.70\) bits.

Math1. Canonical Free-Unit Deduplication & The 66-Node Descent Tree (@gbotrel): Because \(88\%\) of smooth points inside the 12D ellipsoid are unit-associate duplicates \((\varepsilon a, \varepsilon b)\) that produce identical matrix rows, @gbotrel used 6D lattice reduction (LLL) to pick a single canonical \(a\)-representative per unit orbit—skipping \(88\%\) of wasted sieving—and constructed the complete 66-node 12D ball-sieved \(\mathfrak{q}\)-descent tree to find individual logarithms.

Math2. Symmetry-Filtered Schirokauer Maps & Descent Rerandomization (@gbotrel, @cryptogakusei): The even polynomial structure (\(X^2\)) gives a sign symmetry \(\sigma: X \mapsto -X\) that automatically cancels half the unit directions. Keeping only the \(\le 27\) active unit columns simplified linear algebra into a single homogeneous solve, while random auxiliary ideals ensured the descent tree never gets stuck in a loop if it hits a missing factor-base prime twice.

Math3. Certified Lattice Point Counts & The \(p=2\) Murphy-\(\alpha_g\) Fix (@kaankolcu, @mpjunior92, @pepedesigner, @bernardo-lima12): Contributors replaced rough volume approximations with certified discrete lattice-point bounds, separated offline Monte Carlo sampling noise from runtime attack probability, and fixed a missing root-counting factor above \(p=2\) in Murphy’s \(\alpha_g\) to push the score below \(2^{122}\).

Implementation4. Fast \(\pm 1\) Linear Algebra (bw_unit) & Chunked Bucket Recovery (@pepedesigner, @kaankolcu): Experimentally, over \(88\text{–}94\%\) of the nonzero entries in the filtered matrix are simply \(+1\) or \(-1\). Replacing full \(96\)-instruction modular multiplications with \(33\)-instruction additions and subtractions cut linear algebra cost nearly in third, while partitioning the sieve into \(2^{30}\)-cell chunks avoided reading prime buckets in chunks with no survivors.

MathImplementation5. Leaner Relation Excess (\(R_{\text{found}} > 1.1\,U\)) & Lazy Descent ECM (@kaankolcu, @gbotrel, @pepedesigner, @mpjunior92): Proving a tighter unit-deduplication rate inside the descent balls allowed contributors to reduce the main-phase oversieving requirement in our relation equation from \(R_{\text{found}} > 1.3\,U\) down to \(R_{\text{found}} > 1.1\,U\), while stopping ECM cofactorization inside each descent step as soon as the first valid child relation is found.

MathImplementation6. Contiguous Slice Runs (rec7) & Deep Matrix Merging (@kaankolcu, @mpjunior92, @web5200, @ethvs): Instead of running an expensive 6D LLL check on every single \(a\)-slice, rec7 pre-screens slices with a fast list of small units and sieves \(1{,}024\) contiguous slices at a time. Simultaneously, because \(\pm 1\) matrix entries are so cheap, contributors merged rows up to weight \(300\), shrinking the final matrix to just \(3.58\%\) of the factor base \(U\) and breaking the \(2^{120}\) barrier (\(119.48\) bits on Nov 6).

MathImplementation7. Post-06/11 Update I — \(f\)-Only Sieving, Scaled Remainder Trees (SRT), Cofactor Windows & \((72,5)\) Block-Diagonal BW (@gbotrel, @Phoenix0075, @kaankolcu · \(119.48 \to 117.47\) bits): Eliminated \(g\)-side sieving altogether: only the \(f\)-side is sieved, and for the \(\sim 2^{-22.5}\) fraction of cells surviving the \(f\)-threshold, their exact \(576\)-bit \(g\)-norm \(z\) is tested for \(2^{69}\)-smoothness in waves of \(K = 2^{61}\) via a Bernstein Scaled Remainder Tree \((P_g \bmod z)^{2^{10}} \equiv 0 \pmod z\) (paired with a \(K = 2^{39}\) mid-band SRT strip on \((2^{20}, 2^{48}]\)). On the \(f\)-side, ECM runs only when the stripped composite cofactor \(C\) falls in the three valid smoothness windows \(64k < \log_2 C \le 69k\) (\(k \in \{2,3,4\}\), skipping \(\sim 84\%\) of ECM calls), while totally complex signature analysis reduced the \(\sigma\)-odd Schirokauer block to \(21\) columns and introduced \((m,n) = (72,5)\) Block Wiedemann with a shuffled block-diagonal left projection \(X\) and \(5\)-chain interleaved Horner evaluation.

MathImplementation8. Post-06/11 Update II — Non-Monogenic Twisted \(\tau\)-Tower (\(h_2\)), \(\mathfrak{p}_2\)-Coset Sieving & Weight-400 \(\tau\)-Filtering (@gbotrel, @0xplemonade, @kaankolcu · \(117.47 \to 115.70\) bits): Replaced \(h(y)\) with \(h_2(y) = y^6 + 2y^4 - 2y^3 - 2y^2 - 1\) (where \(\mathcal{O}_{K_h}\) is non-monogenic of index \([\mathcal{O}_{K_h} : \mathbb{Z}[y]] = 4\) and uses an explicit integral basis with shifted top polynomials in \(w = y + 1\), \(N(w) = 2\)). This field admits an order-\(2\) automorphism \(\sigma\) and unit \(c \in \mathcal{O}_{K_h}^\times\) (\(c\sigma(c) = -1\)) that lift to an order-\(4\) twisted automorphism \(\tau : (y, x) \mapsto (\sigma(y), cx)\) acting as \(\text{Frobenius}^3\) on \(\mathbb{F}_{p^{12}}\) (\(\log \tau(\mathfrak{P}) \equiv \chi \log \mathfrak{P} \pmod r\) with \(\chi^2 \equiv -1\)). Quotienting by \(\tau\)-orbits of size \(4\) halves the factor base to \(U' = U/2 = 2^{62.451}\), slashes charged Murphy \((\alpha_f, \alpha_g)\) from \((5.15, 0.85)\) to \((0.29, 0.75)\) (with exact per-row \(2\)-adic valuation \(v_2(N_f) \in \{0,6\}\)), skips \(25\%\) of cells via \(\mathfrak{p}_2\)-coset sieving (\(b \notin \mathfrak{p}_2\) whenever \(a \in \mathfrak{p}_2\)), halves the Schirokauer block to \(10\) \(\chi\)-eigenpart columns, and merges the \(\tau\)-quotient matrix to weight \(400\) (\(N/U' \le 2.69\%\), \(3\) owners) with \((m,n) = (144,17)\) Block Wiedemann to reach \(2^{115.6983}\) (\(115.70\) bits).

05 · General Remarks & What Comes NextScope, cross-track scoring & mathematical outlook

All concrete improvements described here apply specifically to BLS12-381 within the CurveSmash benchmark. Because the exact same STNFS codebase powers three tracks—bls12-381-fixed-g1-invert-g2 (FAPI-1), bls12-381-fixed-g2-invert-g1 (FAPI-2), and bls12-381-g1-ecdlp-plain (plain \(G_1\) DLP)—every algorithmic leap simultaneously advances all three leaderboards (with the \(G_1\) track reporting a numerical score \(\log_2(10) \approx 3.32\) bits higher solely because its reference divisor is \(C = 2{,}000\) rather than \(C = 20{,}000\) instructions).
