CurveSmash · BLS12-381 · Special Tower Number Field Sieve

Smashing the Tower Number Field Sieve

How the Tower Number Field Sieve (TNFS) governed progress across three cryptanalysis tracks on BLS12-381; why translating abstract literature estimates into instruction-metered “bits” is not straightforward; and how CurveSmash drove major mathematical and algorithmic advances from a split polynomial tower to the November 2026 frontier.

By Julio de Mello Bezerra and Youssef El Housni

01 · One Sieve to Rule Three TracksWhy a finite-field DLP oracle breaks both curves and pairings

One of the key advantages of general elliptic curves over finite fields is that elliptic curves lack the extra structure necessary for index calculus attacks, which consists of solving the discrete logarithm problem (DLP) for sufficiently many small primes, and uses these solutions to reconstruct a solution to the original DLP. However, certain pairing-friendly elliptic curves, such as BLS12-381, allow one to use their pairing to transfer the DLP on the elliptic curve to the target group, which is a subgroup of a finite field. There, one can perform index calculus successfully, and the best way to do it is via the number field sieve (NFS) and its variants, explained below.

Schematically, BLS12-381 equips three cyclic subgroups \(G_1\), \(G_2\), and \(G_T\) of prime order \(r\) with an optimal ate pairing \(e : G_1 \times G_2 \to G_T \subset \mathbb{F}_{p^{12}}^*\), where \(p\) is a \(381\)-bit prime defining the base field \(\mathbb{F}_p\), and \(r < p\) is a \(255\)-bit prime divisor of the curve order—so \(G_1\), \(G_2\), and \(G_T\) all have prime size \(r \approx 2^{255}\).

Of CurveSmash’s six initial BLS12-381 tracks, three are governed by elliptic-curve group attacks (Pollard’s \(\rho\) and Cheon’s algorithm). The best submissions on the other three tracks are all powered by a single finite-field engine: the Special Tower Number Field Sieve (STNFS) on BLS12-381[2]:

  • Plain \(G_1\) DLP (bls12-381-g1-ecdlp-plain): Given \(P_0 \in G_1\) and \(T = [x]P_0\), recover the \(255\)-bit scalar \(x \in \mathbb{F}_r\).
  • Fixed-Argument Pairing Inversion 1 — FAPI-1 (bls12-381-fixed-g1-invert-g2): Given \(P \in G_1 \setminus \{\mathcal{O}\}\) and \(z \in G_T\), find \(Q \in G_2\) such that \(e(P, Q) = z\).
  • Fixed-Argument Pairing Inversion 2 — FAPI-2 (bls12-381-fixed-g2-invert-g1): Given \(Q \in G_2 \setminus \{\mathcal{O}\}\) and \(z \in G_T\), find \(P \in G_1\) such that \(e(P, Q) = z\).

Attacking \(G_1\) or \(G_2\) directly on the curve requires \(\approx \sqrt{\pi r / 4} \approx 2^{127.25}\) group operations via Pollard’s \(\rho\). However, the optimal ate pairing \(e : G_1 \times G_2 \to G_T \subset \mathbb{F}_{p^{12}}^*\) is bilinear: \(e([a]P, [b]Q) = e(P, Q)^{ab}\). It acts as a bridge that transports problems from the elliptic curve into the \(4569\)-bit extension field \(\mathbb{F}_{p^{12}}^*\)—where index calculus runs orders of magnitude faster.

3. FAPI-2 (Invert \(G_1\) for fixed \(Q \in G_2\)): Symmetrically pick \(P_0 \in G_1 \setminus \{\mathcal{O}\}\), set \(z_0 = e(P_0, Q)\), solve \(z = z_0^x\) in \(G_T\), and return \(P = [x]P_0 \in G_1\).

The CurveSmash challenge[8] on Yukon replaces asymptotic hand-waving with containerized, instruction-metered cryptanalysis. Every submission must supply a complete mathematical proof alongside executable C, AArch64 assembly, and Python verification scripts audited via Callgrind.

02 · From NFS to TNFS to STNFSLifting to a 12-dimensional number field tower

In the finite field \(\mathbb{F}_{p^{12}}^*\), arithmetic wraps around modulo \(p\), destroying any notion of “size” or unique factorization into small primes. Index calculus restores factorization by lifting the problem to characteristic zero, proceeding in four main steps:

  1. Step 1 — Fixing Parameters and Polynomials: We choose a smoothness bound \(B\) and construct two algebraic number rings \(\mathcal{O}_{K_f}\) and \(\mathcal{O}_{K_g}\) (defined by polynomials sharing a common root modulo \(p\)) that admit a common homomorphism down to \(\mathbb{F}_{p^{12}}\). Up in these rings, elements factor uniquely into prime ideals, whose size is measured by their integer norm \(N(\cdot) \in \mathbb{Z}\). We collect all prime ideals of norm \(< B\) on both sides into a factor base of size \(U \approx \text{Li}(B)\) (where the logarithmic integral \(\text{Li}(B)\) comes from the prime number theorem, counting primes up to \(B\)).
  2. Step 2 — Relations and Sieving: Whenever a sieved candidate pair \((a, b) \in \mathbb{Z}^2\) (generalized to \(\mathcal{O}_{K_h}^2 \cong \mathbb{Z}^{12}\) in TNFS below), \(a - bX\), generates principal ideals whose integer norms \(N_f, N_g \in \mathbb{Z}\) factor completely into B-smooth primes (primes \(q < B\)), pushing both factorizations down to \(\mathbb{F}_{p^{12}}^*\) equates the two sides. Because prime ideals upstairs are not elements of \(\mathbb{F}_{p^{12}}^*\) directly, we assign each factor-base ideal \(\mathfrak{p}\) an unknown weight modulo \(r\) called its virtual logarithm \(\log \mathfrak{p}\). Each doubly \(B\)-smooth pair then yields one linear equation modulo \(r\) connecting those \(U\) unknowns. To obtain a full-rank linear system, we must collect more distinct smooth relations \(R_{\text{found}}\) than factor-base unknowns \(U\):
    $$R_{\text{found}} \;=\; V \cdot E \;>\; U \;\approx\; \text{Li}(B),$$
    where \(V\) is the usable sieving volume and \(E\) is Murphy’s E—the average probability over the sieve region that a candidate is simultaneously \(B\)-smooth on both sides. Accurately estimating \(V \cdot E\) versus \(U\)—and above all the joint smoothness probability \(E\)—is by far the hardest part of analyzing the sieve.
  3. Step 3 — Sparse Linear Algebra: Once enough relations are collected, we solve the resulting sparse linear system modulo \(r\) to recover the virtual logarithms of all factor-base ideals. Because standard Gaussian elimination fills in the matrix with nonzero entries and becomes completely infeasible at matrix dimensions around \(2^{56}\text{–}2^{64}\), implementations use the Block Wiedemann algorithm—an iterative solver that finds a null-space vector using only repeated sparse matrix–vector multiplications, keeping memory and computation manageable.
  4. Step 4 — Individual Logarithm Descent: With the factor-base virtual logarithms known, we still need the discrete logarithm of our specific target \(z_T = z_0^x \in G_T\). The descent phase repeatedly rewrites the target (and each resulting intermediate factor) as a product of ideals of progressively smaller norm—using lattice reduction and special-\(\mathfrak{q}\) sieving—until every leaf of the recursion lies inside the precomputed factor base below \(B\). Summing the known virtual logarithms along this tree immediately recovers \(x \pmod r\) and solves the DLP.

Why build a tower? Classical NFS lifts \(\mathbb{F}_{p^{12}}\) in a single degree-\(12\) jump over \(\mathbb{Q}\) and sieves integer pairs \((a, b) \in \mathbb{Z}^2\), forcing \(|a|, |b| \approx 2^{60}\) and causing the norms to explode. The Tower Number Field Sieve (TNFS)[1] mirrors the composite extension \(12 = \eta \cdot \kappa = 6 \cdot 2\)—it first builds a degree-\(6\) base field \(K_h = \mathbb{Q}(y)\), and then sieves degree-\(12\) polynomials \(a - bX\) whose coefficients \(a, b\) are elements of \(\mathcal{O}_{K_h} \cong \mathbb{Z}^6\). Spreading the search volume \(V\) across 12 dimensions shrinks each integer coordinate to \(A \approx 600\) (< 10 bits).

How the curve seed u powers the Special TNFS (STNFS)

Even with a tower (\(\eta = 6, \kappa = 2\)), a generic prime \(p\) forces the top polynomials \(f_y, g_y\) to have huge coefficients (on the order of \(p^{1/3}\)). The Special TNFS (STNFS) exploits the fact that BLS12-381’s \(381\)-bit prime \(p\) is parameterized by a \(64\)-bit integer seed \(u\) via \(3p = \Gamma(u) = u^6 - 2u^5 + 2u^3 + u + 1\). Setting \(g_y(X) = X^2 - uy\) (degree \(2\)) and substituting \(u = X^2/y\) into \(\Gamma(u)\) gives a degree-\(12\) polynomial \(f_y(X) = y^6\Gamma(X^2/y) \bmod h(y)\) whose coefficients drop from \(\sim 2^{127}\) down to 2 bits (\(\|f_y\|_\infty = 2\)).

With @gbotrel’s monogenic Tower 1 polynomial \(h_1(Y) = Y^6 + Y^3 + Y^2 - Y - 1\), the top polynomials are:

$$g_y(X) = X^2 - uy, \qquad f_y(X) = y^6 \Gamma(X^2/y) \bmod h_1(y).$$

(Later in Section 04, Tower 2 switches to non-monogenic \(h_2(y) = y^6 + 2y^4 - 2y^3 - 2y^2 - 1\) with shifted \(w = y + 1\) to unlock an order-4 twisted automorphism.)

Why Infinite Units Create Redundant Ideals: In \(\mathbb{Z}\), the only invertible elements (units) are \(\{\pm 1\}\). In a number ring \(\mathcal{O}_K\), however, there may be infinitely many units \(\varepsilon \in \mathcal{O}_K^\times\). Multiplying any candidate pair \((a, b)\) by a unit \(\varepsilon \in \mathcal{O}_{K_h}^\times\) produces a different integer tuple inside the sieve region that generates the exact same principal ideal—yielding a redundant duplicate relation that adds zero rank to the linear system.

How the Schirokauer Map Resolves Unit Ambiguity: Even when a principal ideal factors uniquely into prime ideals \(\prod \mathfrak{p}_i^{e_i}\), ideal equality only determines the element up to an unknown unit factor \(u \in \mathcal{O}_K^\times\). Computing fundamental units explicitly in degree-72 fields is infeasible; instead, STNFS uses Schirokauer maps—efficiently computable linear maps modulo \(r\) evaluated directly on the polynomial coefficients—to append extra columns to the matrix that automatically absorb all unit contributions modulo \(r\).

03 · Cost Models and Cryptanalytic RulersWhy translating security estimates into “bits” is not straightforward

When Kim and Barbulescu introduced the Extended TNFS (ExTNFS)[1] for composite extension degrees—and combined a degree-\(6\) (sextic) base field with the special polynomial construction for BLS12-381[2] (\(3p = \Gamma(u)\)) to form SexTNFS (or the less sexy STNFS)—two landmark cryptanalytic papers established the pre-CurveSmash security estimates. First, Barbulescu–Duquesne (B-D, 2019)[3] suggested roughly \(122\) bits of security in abstract operations and rough mathematical heuristics (the lower \(117\text{–}120\)-bit figures often quoted in practice came from an unconfirmed draft cited in the NCC Group audit report[4] and related notes[5]). Subsequently, Guillevic–Singh (G-S, 2021)[6] refined the number-theoretic model and reported \(2^{125.27}\) operations (rounded to \(126\) bits).

Comparing those literature numbers directly against CurveSmash[8] scores is an apples-to-oranges exercise. Not only do G-S[6] and CurveSmash measure cost in fundamentally different base units, but a naive constant conversion also breaks down because G-S wrote general heuristic mathematical formulas rather than trying to price concrete CPU instructions rigorously—leaving large modeling differences that require careful interpretation upon closer analysis.

Guillevic–Singh’s Refinements Over Barbulescu–Duquesne · Coprimality ζ(2) & Murphy’s αclick to expand

The following two number-theoretic refinements were introduced by Guillevic and Singh (G-S, 2021)[6] and were completely absent from Barbulescu and Duquesne (B-D, 2019)[3]:

1. Coprimality & the \(1/\zeta_{K_h}(2)\) Factor Shrinking \(V\): In classical NFS over \(\mathbb{Z}\), if a pair of integers \((a,b)\) shares a prime factor \(\ell\), then \((a/\ell, b/\ell)\) generates the exact same relation, so we must restrict the sieve volume to coprime pairs \(\gcd(a,b) = 1\). Because a prime \(\ell\) divides \(a\) with probability \(1/\ell\) and divides both \(a\) and \(b\) independently with probability \(1/\ell^2\), the probability that no prime divides both is given by the Euler product over all primes:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbb{P}(\gcd(a,b) = 1) &= \prod_{\ell\text{ prime}} \left(1 - \frac{1}{\ell^2}\right) = \left(\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{1}{n^2}\right)^{-1} \\ &= \frac{1}{\zeta(2)} = \frac{6}{\pi^2} \approx 60.8\%, \end{aligned}$$

where \(\zeta(s)\) is the Riemann zeta function. Every number field \(K_h\) has an analogous Dedekind zeta function \(\zeta_{K_h}(s)\), and the probability that \((a,b)\) generate coprime ideals works analogously as \(1/\zeta_{K_h}(2)\). This factor directly multiplies (shrinks) the usable sieve volume \(V\) in \(R_{\text{found}} = V \cdot E\), making the attack less effective (whereas B-D ignored this factor).

2. Murphy’s \(\alpha\) (Small-Prime Bias vs. Uniform Random Integers): Values produced by a polynomial resultant \(N_f(a,b)\) do not behave like uniform random integers of the same size when it comes to small prime factors. Murphy’s \(\alpha\) measures this departure from uniform random divisibility across small primes. B-D assumed \(\alpha = 0\) (treating norms as independent uniform random integers), whereas G-S adapted Murphy’s \(\alpha\) to TNFS and showed that on BLS12-381, the rigid seed-derived polynomials have \(\alpha_f + \alpha_g \approx +4.53\) bits—meaning their norms are depleted of small prime factors, lowering \(E\) and adding bits to the attack cost.

1. Different Rulers: Abstract Word Operations vs. Normalized AArch64 Instructions

Recall from Section 02 that the sieve must collect more smooth relations than factor-base unknowns: \(R_{\text{found}} = V \cdot E > U \approx \text{Li}(B)\). In Guillevic–Singh (Section 6.1)[6], total attack bit-security is modeled as \(\log_2(\text{Cost}_{\text{sieve}} + \text{Cost}_{\text{LA}})\) in unscaled abstract operations, starting with the relation-collection cost:

$$\text{Cost}_{\text{sieve}}^{\text{GS}} \;=\; \frac{(2A+1)^{2\eta} \cdot \ln(\ln B)}{2 \cdot \#\text{aut}(h)\,\gcd(\deg f, \deg g)}$$

This connects directly to the search volume \(V\) from Section 02. Here, \(A\) is the integer coordinate half-width of the \(12\)-dimensional box \([-A, A]^{12}\) (since \(2\eta = 12\)), so \((2A+1)^{2\eta}\) counts candidate pairs \((a,b)\) inside the sieve region. Multiplying by \(\ln(\ln B)\) (from Mertens’ Second Theorem) models the average number of primes \(< B\) hitting each cell, where one abstract unit is a single idealized 8-bit sieve array update (\(\ln\ln B \approx 3.813\) updates/cell at \(B = 2^{65.32}\)). Finally, dividing by \(2 \cdot \#\text{aut}(h)\,\gcd(\deg f, \deg g)\) collapses tuples equivalent under sign negation (\(2\)), base-field automorphisms (\(\#\text{aut}(h)\)), and top-polynomial degree symmetries (\(\gcd(\deg f, \deg g)\)).

Once \(R_{\text{found}} > U\) relations are found, G-S[6] price the Block Wiedemann linear algebra phase as:

$$\text{Cost}_{\text{LA}}^{\text{GS}} \;=\; \left\lceil \frac{\log_2 r}{64} \right\rceil \cdot \text{wt} \cdot \left(\frac{\#\mathcal{B}}{\text{flt}}\right)^2$$

Here, \(\lceil (\log_2 r)/64 \rceil = 4\) is the number of 64-bit machine words needed to represent scalars modulo the \(255\)-bit subgroup order \(r\), \(\#\mathcal{B} \approx 2\,\text{Li}(B)\) is the combined two-sided factor-base size, and \(\text{flt}\) and \(\text{wt}\) denote the matrix filtering compression ratio and average row weight after merging (stated in G-S’s text as \(\text{wt} = 200\) and \(\text{flt} = 20\), though their tables and code also fix \(\text{flt} = 9\), where one linear algebra unit models one 64-bit word step of a modular multiplication). Taken together, one abstract G-S operation corresponds to only \(\approx 6.15\) retired AArch64 instructions on average—making G-S’s \(2^{125.27}\) estimate translate naively to roughly \(2^{127.89}\) scalar AArch64 instructions under kernel-measured instruction pricing (as analyzed in the initial Twisted Towers breakdown[7]).

CurveSmash[8], by contrast, audits compiled AArch64 assembly and C kernels via Callgrind to count total retired scalar instructions (Ir), and then divides that raw instruction count by a track-specific group-operation cost \(C\) so that generic Pollard’s \(\rho\) scores \(127.25\) bits in every track:

  • Pairing / FAPI tracks (C = 20,000): Divides pure AArch64 instructions by \(20{,}000 \approx 2^{14.29}\) (the cost of one dense \(\mathbb{F}_{p^{12}}\) multiplication). Dividing by \(20{,}000\) shaves off over \(14\) bits (\(14.29\) bits) from the raw AArch64 instruction count—so \(2^{126.16}\) instructions read as 111.87 on the FAPI scoreboard.
  • Plain \(G_1\) DLP track (C = 2,000): Divides the exact same underlying STNFS instruction count by \(2{,}000 \approx 2^{10.97}\) (one batched affine \(G_1\) point addition), shaving \(10.97\) bits instead. Because of this \(10\times\) divisor difference, the plain \(G_1\) track always reports a score \(\log_2(10) \approx 3.32\) bits higher than the pairing tracks for identical STNFS code.

2. Why a Simple Unit Conversion Fails: Perfect Sieving vs. Real-World ECM & Kernel Costs

Even after accounting for the \(14.29\)-bit divisor, G-S’s formulas[6] cannot be translated via a single constant factor. G-S’s \(V \ln\ln B\) expression assumes perfect sieving all the way up to \(B\): \(100\%\) relation detection, zero memory or bucket-sorting overhead, zero per-slice setup cost, and zero cofactorization or descent work.

In real AArch64 instructions, sieving every prime up to \(B \approx 2^{69}\text{–}2^{70.5}\) is infeasible in finite memory and prohibitively expensive per slice. Practical implementations must stop sieving at a large-prime cutoff around \(2^{63}\text{–}2^{64}\) (and skip very small primes), delegating remaining cofactors to the Elliptic Curve Method (ECM)—an integer-factorization algorithm used in STNFS cofactorization to split un-sieved residual cofactors into primes below \(B\)—alongside remainder trees. When Botrel[7] first priced the attack honestly in AArch64 instructions, these real collection and ECM costs inflated drastically: sieving required \(541.7\) instructions per cell and the full attack required \(2^{137.90}\) AArch64 instructions (\(2^{123.61}\) CurveSmash FAPI units)—over \(10.0\) bits (\(\sim 1{,}030\times\)) higher than G-S’s naive instruction equivalent. That severe ECM and collection inflation only shrank back to sub-dominant levels after subsequent CurveSmash progress[8].

This gap between idealized formulas and instruction-metered execution explains why the \(\sim 2.1\)-bit (\(\sim 4.3\times\)) proximity between Guillevic and Singh’s naive instruction translation (\(2^{127.89}\) AArch64 instructions)[6] and Gautam Botrel’s initial November 8 Twisted Towers ledger (\(2^{129.99}\) AArch64 instructions in the c14 preprint)[7] sparked an important methodological debate—especially as mid-November advances[7][8] have pushed even lower to \(2^{126.16}\) AArch64 instructions. To separate cost-model accounting differences from genuine algorithmic progress cleanly, we first walk through the CurveSmash advances in Sections 04–05 before returning to the full literature comparison in Section 06.

04 · Major Algorithmic & Mathematical AdvancesFrom a split tower to sub 112 bits

CurveSmash’s[8] concrete progress rests on algorithmic and mathematical advances that did not exist in Guillevic and Singh’s 2021 model[6]. Tracing the main developments in mathematical structure and verified bit reduction—from the initial valid baseline (\(2^{123.61}\) CS units / \(2^{137.90}\) AArch64 instructions) down to the latest November 2026 frontier (\(2^{111.87}\) CS units / \(2^{126.16}\) AArch64 instructions)—six core advances drove the \(11.74\)-bit total reduction.

1. Fixing G-S’s Polynomial Tower, Unit-Associate Duplicates, and Sieve Geometry

When CurveSmash contributors first implemented G-S’s Table 11 construction[6], they discovered that its highlighted polynomial \(h_{\text{GS}}(Y) = Y^6 - Y^2 + 1\) fails to build a degree-12 tower over \(\mathbb{F}_p\). Although \(h_{\text{GS}}\) is irreducible over \(\mathbb{F}_p\), its constant term \(h_{\text{GS}}(0) = +1\) forces the field norm \(N_{\mathbb{F}_{p^6}/\mathbb{F}_p}(y) = +1\), which in turn forces \((u\,y)^{(p^6-1)/2} \equiv +1 \pmod p\). Thus \(u\,y\) is a quadratic residue in \(\mathbb{F}_{p^6}\), causing \(g_y(X) = X^2 - u\,y\) to split into two linear factors and collapsing the degree-12 extension down to degree 6. Fortunately, a working polynomial \(h_1(Y) = Y^6 + Y^3 + Y^2 - Y - 1\) (where \(h_1(0) = -1\) gives \(N_{\mathbb{F}_{p^6}/\mathbb{F}_p}(y) = -1\), and because \(p \equiv 3 \pmod 4\) for BLS12-381 makes \(-1\) a quadratic non-residue in \(\mathbb{F}_p\), we conclude \((u\,y)^{(p^6-1)/2} = -1\)) was also listed in G-S’s paper[6] and likewise scored \(126\) bits under G-S’s accounting, as documented by Botrel[7].

A second issue ran deeper than polynomial selection. G-S[6] sampled candidates inside a 12D box \([-A, A]^{12}\) and divided only by \(\{\pm 1\}\), overlooking that the base ring \(\mathcal{O}_{K_h}\) contains infinitely many units \(\varepsilon \in \mathcal{O}_{K_h}^\times\). As explained in Section 02, multiplying a pair \((a, b)\) by any unit \(\varepsilon\) generates the exact same principal ideal, creating widespread duplicate relations inside the box (leaving only about \(2^{-2.81} \approx 1/7\) of counted relations distinct on the valid pair \(h_1\)—roughly \(2.81\) bits short of enough relations at G-S’s published parameters). Restricting candidate sampling to a fundamental domain (fixing a unique representative per unit orbit) removes that oversight. On top of this valid foundation, instead of selecting candidate pairs \((a, b)\) inside a 12-dimensional hypercube \([-A, A]^{12}\) as in B-D and G-S, @gbotrel[7] reshaped the sieving region into a 12D fibred ellipsoid \(\{Q_a(a) + Q_b(b) \le A^2\}\) (where \(Q_a, Q_b\) are positive-definite quadratic forms derived from the complex embeddings of \(K_h\)). Because the basis powers \(1, y, \dots, y^5\) have unequal sizes under the complex embeddings of \(K_h\), the sharp corners of a 12D hypercube extend far along directions where the algebraic norms \(N_f, N_g\) grow needlessly large; rounding the region into an embedding-matched ellipsoid reduces the average norm product by \(\sim 65\) bits at the exact same volume \(V\). Applying 6D LLL canonical deduplication to eliminate the remaining \(\sim 86\%\) unit-associate duplicates then established the valid CurveSmash baseline at \(2^{123.61}\) CS units (\(2^{137.90}\) Ir).

2. Schirokauer Rank Reduction, \(\pm 1\) Unit Linear Algebra, and Lower Relation Excess (123.61 → 119.48 CS)

Recall from Section 02 that relation collection must satisfy \(R_{\text{found}} = V \cdot E > U\). In practice, sieving cannot stop right at \(1.0\,U\): one collects an excess \(R_{\text{found}} = c \cdot U\) (initially \(1.3\,U\)) so structured Gaussian elimination (Markowitz filtering) has enough spare rows to merge away sparse columns without losing rank. Three coupled advances broke the \(2^{120}\) CS barrier[8]:

  • Symmetry-Filtered Schirokauer Maps: Because \(f_y, g_y\) are even in \(X\), the sign involution \(\sigma: X \mapsto -X\) acts as \(\text{Frob}^6\) and forces \(\log \sigma(\mathfrak{P}) \equiv -\log \mathfrak{P} \pmod r\). Restricting Schirokauer unit columns to the \(\le 27\) \(\sigma\)-odd directions eliminated spurious kernel vectors and kept the linear system clean.
  • \(\pm 1\) Unit-Entry Linear Algebra: Contributors observed that \(88\text{–}94\%\) of nonzero entries in the Markowitz-merged matrix are simply \(+1\) or \(-1\). Replacing full modular multiplications with lightweight limb additions and subtractions cut sparse matrix-vector multiplication cost nearly to a third and permitted heavier merges.
  • Contiguous Unit Screening & Reduced Relation Excess (\(1.3\,U \to 1.1\,U\)): Replacing per-slice LLL calls with fast finite-unit screening over contiguous \(a\)-slices and improving individual-logarithm descent allowed the main sieve excess to drop from \(1.3\,U\) to \(1.1\,U\)—directly shrinking the required sieve volume \(V\).

3. Eliminating Two-Sided Sieving: \(f\)-Only Sieving & Batch Remainder Trees (119.48 → 117.47 CS)

Standard TNFS models—including G-S[6]—sieve both the \(f\)-side and the \(g\)-side factor bases across the entire search space. CurveSmash contributors[8] recognized a striking asymmetry: candidates surviving the \(f\)-side smoothness threshold are so rare (\(\sim 2^{-22.5}\) of cells) that eliminating \(g\)-side sieving altogether is much cheaper. Only \(f\) is sieved; each \(f\)-survivor’s exact \(g\)-norm \(z\) is then batch-tested for \(B\)-smoothness using Bernstein’s Scaled Remainder Tree:

$$(P_g \bmod z)^{2^{10}} \equiv 0 \pmod{z}, \qquad P_g = \prod_{p \le B} p^{\lfloor \log_p B \rfloor}$$

which evaluates \(P_g \bmod z\) across large batches of survivor norms simultaneously via FFT product/remainder trees and repeated squaring. On the \(f\)-side, expensive ECM factoring is called only when the remaining composite cofactor’s bit-length lands in valid multi-prime windows (skipping \(\sim 84\%\) of impossible ECM calls). Together with wider Block Wiedemann parameters, this dropped the score to \(2^{117.47}\) CS units.

4. Non-Monogenic Twisted \(\tau\)-Tower (Order-4 Automorphisms) & \(\mathfrak{p}_2\)-Coset Sieving (117.47 → 115.70 CS)

In Section 3.1, G-S’s sieve cost formula[6] divides work by \(\#\text{aut}(h)\) because field automorphisms map smooth ideals to other smooth ideals with identical norms, collapsing both factor-base size \(U\) and matrix dimension. Beyond shrinking \(U\), however, changing the base polynomial also changes small-prime bias (Murphy’s \(\alpha\), explained in the fold in Section 03) and resultant norm sizes—creating a global optimization balance rather than an automatic win.

Switching from \(h_1\) to the non-monogenic polynomial \(h_2(y) = y^6 + 2y^4 - 2y^3 - 2y^2 - 1\) (shifted via \(w = y + 1\) of norm \(2\))[7][8] struck a remarkably favorable point in that trade-off. Alongside an order-2 base automorphism \(\sigma(y)\), \(K_h\) admits a unit \(c \in \mathcal{O}_{K_h}^\times\) satisfying \(c\sigma(c) = -1\), yielding an order-4 twisted automorphism:

$$\begin{gathered} \tau : (y, x) \longmapsto (\sigma(y),\, c\,x), \\ \log \tau(\mathfrak{P}) \equiv \chi \log \mathfrak{P} \pmod r \quad (\chi \equiv p^3,\; \chi^2 \equiv -1) \end{gathered}$$

Quotienting by 4-ideal \(\tau\)-orbits halves the factor base again to \(U' = U/2\) (cutting Schirokauer columns to \(10\)), slashes Murphy’s \((\alpha_f, \alpha_g)\) down to \((0.29, 0.75)\) with exact 2-adic valuation control, skips \(25\%\) of cells via \(\mathfrak{p}_2\)-coset sieving, and cuts linear algebra by roughly \(2\text{–}3\) bits (yielding ~\(0.7\text{–}1.1\) bits net savings in matched comparisons once the twist’s slightly larger discriminant and \(N(w)=2\) coprime penalty are counted)—reaching \(2^{115.70}\) CS units (\(2^{129.99}\) AArch64 instructions in the November 8 c14 ledger[7]). Notably, order-8 symmetries do not exist in \(\text{Gal}(\mathbb{F}_{p^{12}}/\mathbb{F}_p)\), and pursuing larger order-6 or order-12 Galois automorphisms—including conjugation towers explored by Al Aswad, Pierrot, and Thomé (CRYPTO 2026)[9]—currently loses ground overall (\(1.0\) to \(6.3\) bits worse in Botrel’s estimates[7]) because larger discriminants or losing tiny \(f_y\) coefficients inflate norms more than the extra symmetry compensates.

5. Two-Stage Detection & Shared Coset Geometry (Collection to 112.99 CS)

Small primes below \(2^{24}\) generate over \(75\%\) of all hits in G-S’s \(\ln\ln B\) sieve sum[6], yet most cells fail the smoothness threshold anyway. CurveSmash contributors[8] split detection into two stages: Stage A skips all primes \(< 2^{24}\) completely during sieving, while Stage B strips those \(< 2^{24}\) prime factors solely on rare Stage-A survivors using a compact \(3.1\text{ MB}\) Scaled Remainder Tree before invoking \(g\)-batching or ECM.

For mid-range primes \(p \in [2^{24}, 2^{48})\), the sieve relation \(b \mapsto b(s) \bmod p\) is independent of \(a\), and every \(a\)-slice inside the 12D fibred ellipsoid is simply a scaled sub-ellipsoid of the largest slice. Exploiting this shared geometry allows precomputing one sorted coset table per \((p, s)\) (totaling \(2^{111.83}\) bytes of RAM) and replaying hits across slices via simple boundary cuts. Together with class-asymmetric volume scaling into favorable 2-adic slices and raising \(B = 2^{70.5}\), collection drops from \(42.2\) down to \(13.34\) instructions per cell (\(2^{112.99}\) CS units).

6. Product-Form Recorded Pivots & Wide Block Wiedemann (Linear Algebra to 109.95 CS)

Rather than running Markowitz Gaussian elimination until matrix row weights grow unmanageably large, the latest pipeline stops merging early (around mean weight \(200\)) and records the remaining unit-triangular elimination pivots explicitly in product form[7][8]. This compresses the live square matrix dimension down to \(N_l \le 0.00414\,U' \approx 2^{56.00}\)—a \(241.5\times\) reduction over \(\tau\)-orbits (or \(483\times\) over \(\sigma\)-orbits, compared with G-S’s \(20\times\)). Paired with wide asymmetric Block Wiedemann parameters \((m, n) = (8192, 130)\), subset-sum lookup tables over dense small-ideal columns, and truncated-quotient multiplication by \(\chi\) (via its compact \(\sim 192\)-bit representative), linear algebra falls to \(2^{109.95}\) CS units.

7. Higher-Bound Descent & Probabilistic Run Gating (Total to 111.87 CS)

Raising the smoothness bound to \(B = 2^{70.5}\) eases individual-logarithm descent substantially, cutting expected 2-log \(\mathfrak{q}\)-descent work to \(2^{108.86}\) CS units (under \(5\%\) of non-aborted work) while lifting core success probability to \(99.89\%\) (\(2^{113.23}\) CS units). Placing a probabilistic Step 0 Run Gate ahead of the solver—immediately accepting ~\(39.04\%\) of runs and aborting the rest at zero cost to match the \(39\%\) CurveSmash challenge threshold—scales total expected work by \(0.3904\times\) (\(-1.36\) bits) down to \(2^{111.87}\) CS units[8].

Brief Note on Implementation EngineeringAssembly Kernels, Precomputed Lookups & Arithmetic Tuning. Alongside those mathematical ideas, low-level engineering shaved substantial constant factors: hand-written AArch64 assembly kernels for walking high-prime intervals above \(2^{48}\), branchless lower-bound lookups and prefix copies over the precomputed coset replay lists, small subset-sum lookup tables grouping dense orbit columns during matrix-vector products, and tuned FFT/carry-merge routines inside remainder trees and Block Wiedemann projections.

05 · Overview of the STNFS Heuristics & Structural CaveatsExplicitly isolating unproven assumptions and physical bottlenecks

Like all Number Field Sieve cryptanalyses, STNFS cannot rely purely on worst-case mathematical proofs: proving joint smoothness distributions across degree-72 number rings or the exact rank behavior of billion-row sparse matrices over \(\mathbb{F}_r\) remains out of reach. Instead, concrete estimates depend on explicit number-theoretic conjectures and statistical models—heuristics. A major strength of the CurveSmash benchmark is that every unproven assumption is stated explicitly and corroborated by limited, small-scale empirical experiments rather than left implicit. Those heuristic assumptions fall into five modular families:

H1Relation Yield & Smoothness Probability E

Dickman/Murphy Smoothness, 2-Adic Valuations & Two-Stage Retention

Directly supporting the smoothness probability \(E\) in Section 02’s \(R_{\text{found}} = V \cdot E > U\) condition, H1 computes \(E\)—and hence the expected number of relations—primarily via Dickman’s \(\rho\) function (the asymptotic smoothness-probability function, where \(\rho(u) \approx u^{-u}\) gives the fraction of integers near size \(N\) whose prime factors are all below \(N^{1/u}\)). Specifically, H1 assumes that \(f\)- and \(g\)-side norms behave as independent integers once adjusted by exact Kummer–Dedekind Murphy \(\alpha\) values (\(0.29\) and \(0.75\)) and exact 2-adic valuations. It also models Euler-product retention when primes \(< 2^{24}\) are skipped during sieving and stripped in Stage B, along with composite cofactor window probabilities prior to ECM.

H2Sieving Volume V & Lattice Sampling

12D Fibred Ellipsoid Integration & Discrete Lattice Counts

Governing the usable sieving volume \(V\) over the class-asymmetric 12D fibred ellipsoid, H2 estimates overall relation yield via Monte Carlo sampling across independent pre-registered integration pools and large-scale lattice-point acceptance experiments, adding explicit standard-error safety margins to guard against sampling variance.

H3Filtering & Block Wiedemann Linear Algebra

Schirokauer Rank, Product-Form Pivot Compression & Krylov Full Rank

Connecting relation collection to the sparse linear system modulo \(r\), H3 assumes that \(r\) is unramified and coprime to class numbers, and that the \(10\) \(\chi\)-eigenpart Schirokauer columns absorb all unit ambiguity to leave a 1-dimensional kernel. It further extrapolates product-form pivot compression down to \(N_l / U' \le 0.00414\) (\(241.5\times\) reduction over \(\tau\)-orbits) and assumes full Krylov rank for wide \((8192, 130)\) Block Wiedemann with a shuffled block-diagonal left projection.

H4Hardware Costing & Large-Integer Arithmetic

Static Instruction Metering, ±1 Matrix Entries & FFT Scaling

Mapping mathematical steps onto the CurveSmash ruler, H4 converts algorithm loops into static retired AArch64 instructions, bounds the fraction of \(\pm 1\) unit entries in the merged matrix (\(\ge 88\%\)) and clean contiguous slice runs, and extrapolates Schönhage–Strassen FFT arithmetic costs up to the \(\sim 2^{65}\)-limb prime product used in batch smoothness testing.

H5Success Likelihood, Descent & Run Gating

Probabilistic Success Model for Descent & Expected-Work Acceptance Gate

Covering the final phase of Section 02’s pipeline, H5 provides the probabilistic model for the overall likelihood of success: it models the randomized lattice-reduction and special-\(\mathfrak{q}\) descent steps via binomial-type trial distributions up to a high work cap, ensuring a \(99.89\%\) success probability on non-aborted runs ahead of the \(39\%\) Step 0 probabilistic run gate.

Structural Caveats of the Latest Instruction-Metered Ledger

Even as audited instruction counts reached \(2^{126.16}\) retired AArch64 instructions (\(2^{111.87}\) CS units)[8], three structural caveats separate an instruction-metered benchmark score from the physical cost of mounting such an attack in practice.

1. Uncharged Memory Capacity & Communication Bandwidth. Cutting relation collection down to \(13.34\) instructions per cell depends on precomputed sorted coset replay tables totaling roughly \(2^{111.83}\) bytes (\(\approx 4.6 \times 10^{12}\) zettabytes) of memory. Because Callgrind counts only retired scalar CPU instructions, DRAM capacity, cache latency, and cross-node interconnect bandwidth across those replay tables carry zero cost in the score.

2. Extrapolation from Limited Small-Scale Experiments. Because full-scale execution is infeasible, key linear algebra heuristics—including \(241.5\times\) product-form matrix compression and full-rank \((8192, 130)\) Block Wiedemann with shuffled block-diagonal projections—can only be verified on limited synthetic matrices at scales \(2^{12}\text{–}2^{23}\) and are extrapolated across more than 40 powers of two to \(U' \approx 2^{63.92}\) without an end-to-end execution of all four phases together.

3. Expected Work vs. Worst-Case Tail. Optimizing expected cost behind a \(39\%\) probabilistic acceptance gate at Step 0 brings the headline score down to \(2^{111.87}\) CS units (\(2^{126.16}\) Ir). However, individual non-aborted runs rely on a worst-case descent cap reaching \(2^{121.9}\) CS units—more than \(10\) bits above the expected-work headline—trading deterministic run-time certainty for expected efficiency.

Aside on Prime Bounds (GRH). Neither the earliest CurveSmash submissions nor the later submissions from Plain P11 onward rely on the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis (GRH). There was only a brief period during the project when a few intermediate submissions assumed GRH to bound prime counts inside remainder trees, which was promptly dropped once contributors observed that unconditional Rosser–Schoenfeld inequalities suffice. While writing this, it seems GRH is now proven and therefore unconditional.

06 · Interpreting the Progress: Literature vs. CurveSmashReconciling Guillevic–Singh (2021) directly against the CurveSmash frontier

Now that Sections 04 and 05 have detailed both the algorithmic advances and the heuristic caveats behind CurveSmash’s latest score (\(2^{111.87}\) CS units / \(2^{126.16}\) AArch64 instructions)[8], we can interpret how Guillevic and Singh’s (G-S, 2021) estimate[6] compares against the modern instruction-metered frontier.

Balanced View: Coincidence vs. Expected Order of Magnitude

At first glance, G-S’s naive instruction translation (\(2^{127.89}\) AArch64 instructions)[6] and the latest CurveSmash frontier (\(2^{126.16}\) AArch64 instructions)[8] appear separated by only \(1.73\) bits. Yet comparing G-S’s 2021 formula directly against CurveSmash reveals that this ballpark agreement conceals two opposing stories.

Perspective 1 · Bottom-Up Accounting Decomposition

Why the Proximity Masks Large Opposing Differences

  • Split Polynomial & Unit-Duplicate Oversight: G-S’s printed polynomial \(h_{\text{GS}}(Y) = Y^6 - Y^2 + 1\) splits \(g_y\) over \(\mathbb{F}_{p^6}\), and on a valid polynomial pair G-S’s hypercube box overcounts unit-equivalent multiples \((\varepsilon a, \varepsilon b)\) by \(2^{2.81}\). Fixing that 2.81-bit relation deficit—and re-costing the extra sieving and linear algebra needed to make it up—adds +3.80 bits of work (or +3.32 bits net after switching to a fundamental domain, an idea from CurveSmash).
  • Omitted Real Attack Phases: G-S assigned zero cost to ECM cofactorization, sieve-threshold losses, filtering excess, Schirokauer columns, and the individual logarithm descent. Pricing an unoptimized valid attack in instructions requires explicitly accounting for these steps, which is not trivial. The first accepted submission in CurveSmash raised the total cost by over \(9\) bits mostly due to this.
  • Uncalibrated Linear Algebra Weighting: By charging \(800\) abstract units per sparse matrix row alongside \(3.81\) idealized units per sieve cell, G-S overweighted linear algebra relative to sieving. Re-pricing with realistic kernels and re-optimizing parameters breaks the balance between linear algebra and relation collection costs from G-S.
  • Genuine CurveSmash Algorithmic Progress: Many significant mathematical and algorithmic gains are hidden when comparing with G-S’s abstract model and may represent two-digit bits improvement depending on the accounting approach—driven by order-4 twisted towers, \(f\)-only batch remainder trees, two-stage detection, coset replay lists, product-form pivots, and expected-work gating.

Perspective 2 · Macro-Calibration & Asymptotic Anchoring

Why G-S Still Landed in the Right Security Neighborhood

  • Explicit Calibration Against Real DLP Records: G-S[6] did not choose \(V \ln\ln B\) or their filtering constants arbitrarily—they anchored their simulation parameters to empirical experience from cado-nfs finite-field discrete logarithm records, verifying that their unscaled BLS12 curve intersected the calibrated Special-NFS asymptote near the 128-bit security tier.
  • Sub-Dominance of Descent & ECM Once Tuned: G-S omitted individual-logarithm descent and ECM cofactorization because at \(B \approx 2^{65}\text{–}2^{70.5}\), post-linear-algebra phases should become secondary once well engineered. Indeed, after CurveSmash’s optimizations, relation collection accounts for roughly \(85\%\) of total work (vs. \(\sim 10\%\) for linear algebra), while expected 2-log descent stays under \(5\%\), vindicating that intuition.
  • Intended Hardware-Agnostic Comparative Baseline: G-S explicitly stated that their model omitted memory and parallelization costs to provide an architecture-independent comparison across pairing families. Placing BLS12-381 around \(2^{125.27}\) abstract operations placed it in the right cryptographic security neighborhood, even before physical instruction metering.
Neutral Synthesis. Both perspectives highlight essential truths. Strategically, Guillevic and Singh’s 2021 model[6] anchored BLS12-381 in the right cryptographic ballpark. Numerically, however, translating G-S directly into AArch64 Instr. revealed oversights and submissions supplied explicit constructions for omitted steps. Further, CurveSmash’s latest frontier goes beyond merely redressing the literature, but achieves genuine mathematical and engineering progress. Translating this progress into numbers depends on interpretation and oversimplifies the picture. (For an attempt at a more exact numerical comparison, readers interested in the complete itemized instruction ledgers can consult Twisted Towers[7].)

07 · Cross-Track Scoring & Concluding RemarksShared oracle, distinct track rulers

All concrete improvements analyzed here apply specifically to BLS12-381 within the CurveSmash benchmark[8]. Because the exact same STNFS codebase powers three tracks—bls12-381-fixed-g1-invert-g2 (FAPI-1), bls12-381-fixed-g2-invert-g1 (FAPI-2), and bls12-381-g1-ecdlp-plain (plain \(G_1\) DLP)—every mathematical and implementation advance simultaneously moves all three leaderboards. The plain \(G_1\) track simply reports a score \(\log_2(10) \approx 3.32\) bits higher (\(2^{115.19}\) vs. \(2^{111.87}\)) because its normalizing divisor is \(C = 2{,}000\) instructions rather than \(C = 20{,}000\).

Moreover, the same STNFS attack can be transported to the BN254 curve[7], where it is even stronger and likewise dominates the two BN254 tracks recently added in CurveSmash[8].

Closing Perspective. Separating cost-model accounting errors from algorithmic progress clarifies the entire arc of BLS12-381 cryptanalysis: Guillevic and Singh’s 2021 model[6] anchored BLS12-381 in the right security neighborhood even while relying on heuristic formulas, whereas CurveSmash’s instruction-metered rigor[7][8] uncovered those hidden accounting gaps and spurred genuine mathematical advances—from order-4 twisted towers and Scaled Remainder Trees to two-stage coset replay and product-form Block Wiedemann—that have now brought verified expected instruction counts below even G-S’s unadjusted baseline.
